The Story

A girl who draws life,
teaches language, and
dreams of the sea.

I have lived in Laos since October 2015 — longer than I've lived anywhere else. My parents chose service over stability, and I grew up watching that choice shape everything our family values. This page is not a summary I made for applications — it is a record I kept for myself, finally made visible.

Two things have stayed with me for as long as I can remember: I draw constantly, and I am fascinated by living creatures. At seven I sculpted clay dogs by the boxful. At twelve I won a national art award in Korea. At fifteen I animated a short film frame by frame. Today I fill sketchbooks with animals and study the biology behind them. My art teacher and I have begun talking seriously about scientific illustration as a career path — the place where biological accuracy and visual craft meet.

I also want to work in aquariums. Not as a casual interest, but as a professional goal — in education, exhibit design, or conservation communication. This portfolio documents the experiences that make that goal legible.

My academic path reflects that same adaptability. I graduated from a Lao public elementary school — passing the national exit exam with a government-certified diploma. When COVID closed my secondary school mid-year, I transitioned to American homeschool curricula: first Abeka, then BJU, restarting at 9th grade to build a stronger foundation. Today my GPA sits at 3.7–3.9. The transcript tells a story not of privilege, but of persistence across three educational systems and two languages.

Hangul Journal · Session 8

Graduation Day —
Paper Airplanes & a Korean Song

On May 22, 2026, the 23rd graduating class of Dindee Nursery and Kindergarten School performed 비행기 ("Airplane") — a Korean folk song — for their families and teachers. The performance was the culmination of three months of weekly sessions, eight in total, each one self-designed and adapted to a classroom of twenty-five Lao kindergarteners with no prior Korean exposure.

Every lesson plan, pronunciation note, and classroom instruction was written directly in Lao script on the whiteboard — not phonetic transliteration for a foreign teacher's reference, but native-alphabet annotation for children who could read it. That is what localized educational service means: not delivering content in spite of the language barrier, but working through it, in the children's own script.

The paper airplanes the children flew were each decorated by hand. When asked to draw butterfly wings, many instead drew hearts, circles, and their own names. Not a deviation from instruction — a declaration of authorship. Every plane that flew on that stage carried something the curriculum didn't plan: the child's own visual language.

Localized Educational Service

Lesson plans handwritten in Lao script. Korean phonemes glossed in the children's native alphabet. Teaching through the language barrier, not around it — this is what cross-cultural pedagogy looks like at the board level.

Creative Reinterpretation · Art-Bio-Service

Children drew hearts and circles on their planes instead of butterfly wings — exercising creative agency over materials meant to carry their voice. The heart motif, universal across cultures, became the child's chosen language for ownership and joy.

Service in Action

Interpreter Mission,
Xieng Khouang

Xieng Khouang is a remote province in northern Laos — hours from the capital, with few resources and even fewer outside visitors. Since 2023, I have joined a Korean church mission team to Xieng Khouang three times (2023, 2024, 2025), serving as the sole Korean-Lao interpreter.

Each mission was based at Somvatthana School. Over 3–7 days on-site, I interpreted between Korean team members and Lao school staff and students — enabling classroom instruction, school events, and community activities. The role demanded cultural sensitivity, real-time judgment, and the ability to make both sides feel genuinely heard.

Across three trips, I contributed approximately — hours of active service. The school issued an official verification letter in Lao — rare documentation that formally confirms this role.

Beyond interpreting, I picked up a brush. The mission team painted two large murals on the school walls — an ark of animals crossing open water, and a garden scene of children among trees and flowers. It was physical, communal work: no translation needed, just shared labor under the same sun. For someone who draws animals in sketchbooks, painting them life-sized on a school wall felt like a different kind of fluency.

✦ Personal Account · In Her Own Words

What Kept Bringing Me Back

Three years. Three trips on a nine-hour dirt road. Each time, I went back because I couldn't stop thinking about the children I met the year before.

Nine hours on a dirt road is a long time to have second thoughts.

The first time I went to Xieng Khouang, I had signed up to interpret for a Korean church mission at Somvatthana School — a kindergarten in the mountains of northern Laos. My Lao was conversational. I had never done anything like this before. Somewhere around hour four of the bus ride, with the road getting narrower and the potholes getting deeper, I started wondering whether I had made a mistake.

I hadn't. The school festival was already running when we arrived. The children walked out in costumes they had made from used paper and trash bags — a full fashion show, staged on a concrete courtyard, with no budget and no self-consciousness. I had come to interpret words. I ended up watching children teach me something about what creativity actually requires: not resources, but the decision to make something out of whatever is in front of you.

I went back the following year. The reason, if I'm honest, was simple: I kept thinking about their faces.

By the third trip, the work had changed shape. Each mission lasted three to seven days on-site. I interpreted in real time — across classrooms, teacher meetings, school events — which sounds manageable until you're trying to render a joke from one culture that has no equivalent in another. Interpretation, I learned, is not about words. It is about judgment: reading the room, reading the speaker, deciding in half a second what the other language actually needs.

Sixty hours of that work, across three years. The road never got shorter. But by the third time, I stopped noticing. That, I think, is what service actually looks like — not a single gesture of goodwill, but the practice of showing up to the same place, for the same people, until the distance starts to feel like belonging.

✦ Reflection · May 22, 2026 · Session 8

The Work Before the Performance

On Dignity of Labor, Service as Method, and Berea College's Work Program

The ceremony began at 9:00 a.m. Our performance — the last item on the program — went on at 11:30. Two and a half hours of preparation for eight minutes on stage. That ratio is the whole story.

The ceremony began at 9:00 a.m. Our performance — the Korean song, the paper airplanes, the eight minutes the children had been rehearsing for three months — was the last item on the program. It went on at 11:30. Two and a half hours of preparation for eight minutes on stage.

That ratio is the whole story. Those two and a half hours looked like this: writing Lao script on a whiteboard, folding paper into airplanes, distributing decorated props to twenty-five five-year-olds who had already decided the airplanes were more interesting than the instructions, and standing at the side of the stage counting children who kept wandering off their marks. Waiting. Redirecting. Waiting again. The performance lasted eight minutes. The work is everything that makes eight minutes possible — and it begins at 9:00 a.m., when the audience is still at home.

I have been thinking, over the past several months, about what Berea College means when it says every student works. Not "every student receives financial aid in exchange for campus labor" — the Work Program is philosophically older and more serious than that framing. Berea's founders argued that labor is not a price to pay for education. It is education. The discipline of showing up, of doing the unglamorous task well, of understanding that no work which serves others is beneath you — these are not byproducts of the program. They are its thesis.

I have been living something like that thesis, without knowing it had a name. The visible part of my service record is the Hangul curriculum — eight sessions, self-designed, twenty-five students, a Korean folk song performed at a Lao kindergarten graduation. The invisible part is the session where I helped set up lunch because the teacher was alone. The part where I cleaned up after a balloon game no one won. Today, the part where I carried paper airplanes up a stage staircase and placed them, one by one, into children's hands. That labor does not appear on any certificate. It also does not need to.

Berea asks its students to labor not because labor builds character in the abstract, but because shared work builds community in the specific. I do not know what my work assignment will be at Berea. But I know what I have already learned about work from a classroom in Vientiane: the children who fly the planes on stage are not separate from the person who folded them the night before. The service is not the performance. The service is the labor that makes the performance true.
