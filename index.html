The Story

A girl who draws life,
teaches language, and
dreams of the sea.

I have lived in Laos for 11 years — longer than I've lived anywhere else. My parents chose service over stability, and I grew up watching that choice shape everything our family values.

Two things have stayed with me for as long as I can remember: I draw constantly, and I am fascinated by living creatures. At seven I sculpted clay dogs by the boxful. At fifteen I animated a short film frame by frame. Today I fill sketchbooks with animals and study the biology behind them. My art teacher and I have begun talking seriously about scientific illustration as a career path — the place where biological accuracy and visual craft meet.

I also want to work in aquariums. Not as a casual interest, but as a professional goal — in education, exhibit design, or conservation communication. This portfolio documents the experiences that make that goal legible.

My academic path reflects that same adaptability. I graduated from a Lao public elementary school — passing the national exit exam with a government-certified diploma. When COVID closed my secondary school mid-year, I transitioned to American homeschool curricula: first Abeka, then BJU, restarting at 9th grade to build a stronger foundation. Today my GPA sits at 3.7–3.9. The transcript tells a story not of privilege, but of persistence across three educational systems and two languages.

Service in Action

Interpreter Mission,
Xieng Khouang

Xieng Khouang is a remote province in northern Laos — hours from the capital, with few resources and even fewer outside visitors. Since 2023, I have joined a Korean church mission team to Xieng Khouang three times (2023, 2024, 2025), serving as the sole Korean-Lao interpreter.

Each mission was based at Somvatthana School. Over 3–7 days on-site, I interpreted between Korean team members and Lao school staff and students — enabling classroom instruction, school events, and community activities. The role demanded cultural sensitivity, real-time judgment, and the ability to make both sides feel genuinely heard.

Across three trips, I contributed approximately 60 hours of active service. The school issued an official verification letter in Lao — rare documentation that formally confirms this role.

Beyond interpreting, I picked up a brush. The mission team painted two large murals on the school walls — an ark of animals crossing open water, and a garden scene of children among trees and flowers. It was physical, communal work: no translation needed, just shared labor under the same sun. For someone who draws animals in sketchbooks, painting them life-sized on a school wall felt like a different kind of fluency.

✦ Personal Account · 30-Minute Summary

Three Reasons the Road Was Worth It

I volunteered for translation service at Xiang Khuang, Laos, for three years. I worked for the kindergarten, and other students and a pastor who are from Korea church. I translate a special Korean class and moderator's words during a kindergarten festival.

The first reason I went to Xiang Khuang was curiosity. Never in my life visited a local kindergarten, so I decided to watch them with my own eyes, by participating as an interpreter. The first thing I saw when I rode a bus was a dirt road. The road to Xiang Khuang was so hard and long that it even made me regret my decision every time. It was a nine hour full trip; and finally arrived.

Second, I saw children. Unlike kids in the capital, the children are very pure and sincere. They thanked me genuinely, easily expressed their feelings, and were very creative. The moment that I can see their creativity was when the main event of the school festival began. They made a cloth out of used paper and trash can like real fashion show clothing.

Third, I experienced the wonderful nature of Xiang Khuang. It has a large plain that is filled with a huge jar called Plain of Jars. Also the beauty of the sky was wonderful. Every morning it showed me a cloud's work of art, and every afternoon the sky was filled with golden and orange sunset.

That's the three main reasons why the trip to Xiang Khuang was highly rewarding to me. If I didn't go there I might miss all of the things that are a great valuable knowledge to me.

Looking Forward

What Laos Taught Me
About the Ocean

01

Ecosystems Need Translators

The ocean's crisis is partly a communication crisis. Scientists know what's happening. But the people who can change it don't always speak the language. I grew up being that bridge — and I intend to keep building it.

02

Distance Creates Depth

Being landlocked made me love the ocean more, not less. Absence sharpened my attention. The same is true for science communication: the best explainers are not always insiders. Sometimes they're people who had to seek it out.

03

Service Is the Method

My family chose to live with less so others could have more. Sixty hours interpreting in Xieng Khouang. Weekly Hangul lessons at a Vientiane kindergarten. Painting school walls by hand. Service is not a line on my résumé — it is the method. Labor teaches what textbooks cannot: that showing up, repeatedly, is the prerequisite for every kind of understanding. That orientation shapes how I think about conservation, education, and the aquarium career I am building.
